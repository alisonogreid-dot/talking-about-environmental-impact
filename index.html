How Do We Talk About Environmental Impact?

Every environmental story has a villain, a victim, and a voice telling the story. This week we start noticing who's talking — and what they leave out.

Content What is the role humans have played in environmental damage? Skills Using lenses to view things at different levels (individual, local, global) Sessions Thursday & Thursday — Provocation, Case Study, Counterfactual

01The Lorax as Provocation

We begin with a story that picks a side. Dr. Seuss didn't write The Lorax as a balanced report — he wrote it to make you feel something about trees, responsibility, and the cost of ignoring warnings. That makes it a very specific kind of text: one that uses narrative (story, characters, emotion) to argue a point.

As you watch, pay attention not just to what happens but to how the story makes you feel about what happens. Who does the story want you to blame? Who does it want you to sympathise with? These choices are deliberate.

Keep This in Mind

The Lorax is one-sided on purpose. The Once-ler has economic reasons for what he does — but the story doesn't give those reasons any respect. That's not a flaw. It's a rhetorical choice. By the end of this unit, you'll know the name for this kind of writing and you'll be able to do it yourself.

02From Fiction to Reality: The Aral Sea

The Lorax is fiction. But the pattern it shows — people making decisions about resources, consequences building up, warnings ignored — has played out in the real world, sometimes at staggering scale.

Case Study
The Aral Sea

The Aral Sea was once the world's fourth-largest freshwater lake. Beginning in the 1960s, the Soviet Union diverted rivers feeding the lake to irrigate cotton fields in Central Asia. The decision was made by a centralised government, thousands of kilometres away, for economic reasons. The people who lived near the lake had no say.

The lake shrank to 10% of its original size. Salinity rose from 10 g/l to over 100 g/l. Fish died. Toxic sediments, previously underwater, became exposed and blew across communities. The local climate changed. Biodiversity collapsed. Livelihoods built around fishing and agriculture disappeared.

International collaborations are now working to restore water flows and introduce sustainable land management — but the damage took decades and a complete political system change to even begin addressing.

Notice the Pattern

Who made the decision? A distant government. Who suffered the consequences? Local communities. Who had the power to stop it? That's a governance question — and it's one we'll keep returning to throughout this unit.

03What If…? The Aral Sea Counterfactual

A counterfactual asks: what if a key decision had been different? It's a way of testing whether the outcome was inevitable or whether it was the result of specific choices by specific people (or governments, or systems).

This matters because if the Aral Sea disaster was the result of decisions, then different decisions could have prevented it. And if different decisions could have prevented it, then we can ask: what kind of governance, rules, or power structures would have led to those better decisions?

Thursday Aral Sea Counterfactual — Pairs ▼

In pairs, choose one key turning point in the Aral Sea story and rewrite the outcome by changing a critical decision or event.

Steps

  1. Identify your turning point. When was the moment where things could have gone differently? (e.g. the decision to divert the rivers, the scale of irrigation, the lack of environmental monitoring, the political system that prevented local input…)
  2. Change the decision. What happens instead? Be specific — don't just say "they didn't do it." What alternative approach do they take?
  3. Trace the consequences. Use your T-chart from yesterday. Which chain of impacts would be broken or changed? What new outcomes emerge?
  4. Justify. Why is your alternative plausible? What would have needed to be true (politically, economically, socially) for your alternative to happen?
Keep it focused

This is a 20–25 minute task. You're writing a paragraph or two, not an essay. The goal is to practise thinking about decisions and their consequences — the same skill you'll use in a bigger way tomorrow.
